November 13, 2022:

By now, I think you all know my mom died on Friday early afternoon. The nurses and doctors at McClure did their best to keep her comfortable. Pain management was a moving target, requiring more frequent and higher dosages of Dilaudid. When I arrived at McClure on Friday morning, I knew she would be gone by the end of the day. I could tell by her breathing and how difficult it was becoming to manage her pain. I sat by her bedside and read her some of the cards sent by family and friends over the last year. I don’t know if she heard them but it comforted me to be reminded of how much she was loved and appreciated.

I thought I’d be able to get through the day alone but I just couldn’t. I texted Joe at 9:30 AM and asked him to come to McClure. About 10 minutes later, I got a text from my friend Maureen letting me know she was on her way up from Concord, MA and about an hour away from McClure. Having just lost her mom earlier this year, she knew when I talked to her on Thursday that the end was near and that I wouldn’t want to be alone. She knew what I needed before I did. She also knew that if she asked me if I wanted her to come up, I’d tell her no. So, she decided not to ask and just hope I wouldn’t be mad at her. Of course I wasn’t. My friend Amy was supposed to meet with her manager for the job she is starting on Monday. As soon as she heard I was allowing people to be there, she canceled her meeting and jumped in the car. Blessed isn’t a word I throw around lightly or really ever, but that’s what I was to have these 3 people by my side on the worst day of my life. 

My mom and I never really talked about the end. I knew she didn’t want to die at home and I remember her saying she didn’t want me to have to see her dead body. My plan was to say my goodbye and leave the room until she was gone. After spending some time with Amy, Joe, and Maureen in the family room, I went back into my mom’s room to see how she was doing. I realized her breathing had changed again. There was a longer pause between breaths and her breathing was more shallow. I could hear the I “death rattle” I’d been warned about by every hospice nurse I’ve ever encountered. It’s a sort of gurgling sound that’s caused by a build up of saliva in the airways. She wasn’t moaning or yelping in pain like she had been earlier in the day and she didn’t seem to be in distress or uncomfortable. I sat by her bed and just started talking to her softly. I told her that it was OK to go. That I’d be OK and so would the rest of the family. I told her that I put together forget-me-not seed packets for her remembrance party and that Joe and I would take seeds with us on every trip we take from now on and plant seeds in her memory. At one point she opened one eye. It was unfocused at first but then she seemed to be looking at me. Maybe for a minute or two. It was the first time she’d shown any sign of being aware of her surroundings since Tuesday night. I don’t know if she really knew I was there or understood but I kept nonstop babbling. I don’t remember most of what I said. She closed her eye. Maybe that was my cue to shut up, but I kept jabbering. I don’t know how I got on the subject but as she took her last breath, I was asking her if she remembered the dog, Fluffy, we had for a short time when I was a kid and how he used to eat the shower curtain.

Again, the staff at McClure was incredible. They were just so kind and respectful. They dressed her and brushed her hair. Put fresh flowers in the room, opened a window, and even lit a candle.

This will be my last entry on this page. I don’t have the words to thank you for your support and encouragement over the last year. I would be a broken person with out you all. I’m a little cracked, but with time I’ll heal. Without you, the damage would have been irreparable.   

With love and gratitude,

Christa

November 10, 2022: Unresponsive but Resting Comfortably

A year ago today we first heard “pancreatic cancer.” While it took 6 weeks to get the definitive diagnosis, this is the day we got the news that there was a mass on her pancreas. After we learned she couldn’t tolerate chemo in January 2022, we were given a prognosis of 3-6 months. As she often does, she defied the odds.

Yesterday she was difficult to rouse in the morning. We were hoping it was just a temporary crash after an adrenaline filled day but that is not the case. As the day progressed yesterday, she stopped waking up at all or responding to stimuli. We tried pulling back on her anti-anxiety/sedation meds to see if that would help rouse her but it didn’t. This morning she is about the same. Unresponsive but her breathing is mostly steady with an occasional attempt to cough every 15 minutes or so. I wouldn’t categorize it as ‘labored’ breathing at this point. Her neck muscles are not straining when she takes a breath. They are giving her pain and anti-anxiety medication and repositioning her regularly so she continues to be comfortable. They also make sure her mouth doesn’t get too dry. They gave her a bed bath this morning and changed her sheets, which required a lot of maneuvering. If she was in any pain, they believe they would have detected it during those activities. One of the nurses said this often happens right after a patient arrives. He/she finally feels relaxed enough to begin to let go and start the dying process. I take comfort in knowing that she knew she was in a safe and comfortable environment and would be well cared for her remaining days.

I talk to her a bit but if she’s hearing me, she doesn’t acknowledge it in anyway. I honestly think I’m annoying her more than anything else, so I mostly sit quietly and every once and awhile remind her that I’m here.

I will let you know if anything changes but I’ve been told not to hope for another rally.

November 9, 2022: At McClure

She arrived at McClure by private ambulance at about 11:50 AM on Tuesday. She did not have a good experience. She was uncomfortable and vomited at least once. (She told me she is dying at McClure because she’s never taking another ambulance ride again!) When she arrived, her intake nurse, Marty, assessed and oriented her. She was wonderful. Her interaction with my mom was nice to see. She was very kind and explained everything. Marty realized quickly that my mom is still very bright even though her verbal response time is slower. Marty was conversational but made sure my mom was comprehending without being condescending. In addition to an RN, she also has a nursing assistant assigned to help with her care and there are a zillion volunteers here. I met her doctor as well and we reviewed her care plan and medication schedule. The doctor was also respectful and patient.

The room is great. Huge and clean with lots of seating, lighting options, and storage. It has two huge sunny windows. She has a comfy bed, she liked the sheets, which says a lot since she’s picky, and the TV is a decent size. They gave her a wrist watch-like device to use as a call button so it doesn’t get lost/hidden in the bed sheets. They have lots of interesting tech. She has a huge private bathroom, with tons of shelving for her preferred toiletries. The staff here have been amazing about proactively checking in and repositioning her for comfort. We both feel like we can call them at any time for any reason and they are truly happy to be helping. I’ve been able to watch several shifts interact with her and they are very consistent. They tell her what they are going to do and wait for her consent before moving her body. They are just so patient and respectful. You’d think my mom was their only patient and they had nothing else in the world to do but make sure she is comfortable and her needs are met. I couldn’t imagine a better level of care than what I’ve seen so far at McClure.

She was awake for most of the day yesterday. and really sharp. I think the clinicians were expecting her to be a lot worse since VNH had put her on there 7-day list. They do everything for her here, including getting her drinks, making sure she has plenty of ice cold water. I serve no purpose here. They make sure what she needs is in reach before they leave the room. One of the volunteers made her a custom blended smoothie – you can ask for them at any time – and she said it was good but requested more ice cream next time. LOL. She actually ate some real food for dinner! She had some pot roast with pasta, a garden salad, and a big dish of chocolate ice cream. While she didn’t clean her plate, she ate enough for me to know the food was decent. She wanted to smoke after dinner. They put together a smoking plan where I take her outside to smoke but we still need to figure out how to get her out of bed.  

Today we are supposed to do a mobility assessment and get her out of bed but she doesn’t want to wake up. I think yesterday was such an adrenaline filled day that she’s sort of crashed today. She will wake up enough to say “No” or “OK” but not enough to interact. She declined breakfast and a bath. She is on the same medication and same schedule and dosing as I’d been giving her at home, so I don’t think they are over medicating her. I think she’s just really tired. I’m hoping she’ll rally a bit later in the day.

I found out earlier today that we were approved for a discount on the room and board rate. Instead of $400 per day, we are only paying $240 per day. I feel like I just got the bargain of the century. i would have had zero reservations about paying $400 per day for the kind of care I saw yesterday and today. 

Ms. Effie spent her first night in Essex Junction. So far, she is not a fan. Effie is staying in the master bedroom and it will be a while before she meets her little sisters. Emmie is desperate to meet her big sister. She meows outside the door and sticks her little paw under the door. Effie doesn’t appreciate the warm welcome. She’s scared and very protective of her food. She growls while she eats, even if I’m sitting with her. Ellie is mostly uninterested in her sister. She’s just her normal zen self. She’s probably the only one in the house that’s relaxed at the moment!

November 6, 2022: Home Again

She came home from the Jack Byrne Center (JBC) at around 12:30 PM on Saturday by ambulance. Maryellen was here when she arrived. The first thing she did was have a cigarette in bed. She doesn’t have the strength to light the cigarette so I have to do this for her. I still feel nauseated from the first one I light! This is horrifying to me but if I watch her like a hawk, I guess it’s ok. I am going to cut her cigarettes in half before I give them to her so they are shorter and she gets through them faster.

My mom’s strength has declined significantly in the last week. She is no longer ambulatory, meaning she cannot get out of bed. She doesn’t have the strength to stand. She already has the beginnings of a bedsore that we are treating with adhesive foam Maryellen put in a foley catheter for urination. padding. Because she has so little strength it is impossible for me to move and reposition her without causing her pain on my own. Joe came down late Saturday evening. Even with the two of us, getting her to the toilet for a bowel movement would take so much time, she’s likely to have an accident on the floor, so she has a diaper. Twice now we’ve moved her and she’s howled in pain. We will get better at it.

Maryellen was at the house at 10:30 this morning as well. After reviewing my log of interactions over Saturday day and night, Maryellen strongly urged me to keep her on a strict 4-hour regimen of pain medication and anti-anxiety medication. I am giving it a try. There was some back and forth on whether to use morphine or Dilaudid/hydromorphone for pain. JBC put her on Dilaudid but they took her off because they were under the impression that hospice wouldn’t prescribe Dilaudid. No one knows where they got that idea but it’s not true. My mom prefers the Dilaudid, so Maryellen ordered that in liquid form (arriving on Monday) and until then I’m mashing up tablets that I picked up today and dissolving them in water. Because my mom was very agitated Saturday night, even after the Lorazepam, Maryellen decided to switch her to Haloperidol.  She seems maybe a bit over sedated so we will re-assess on Monday evening.  The Haloperidol may be too strong.

Since she’s been home, the only thing she will eat is ice cream smoothies. I got the recipes for the ones she liked at JBC and have made a couple to her satisfaction. We are not sure she can eat solid food. Maryellen thinks that it won’t be long before she has trouble swallowing.

In addition to the RN (either Maryellen or Megan) coming once a day, we also have an HHA or LPN coming once a day as well. Katie, an LPN was here yesterday while Maryellen was here and was just here again this afternoon. She helped me reposition her, walked me through emptying the catheter again, and checked her bottom. My mom refused a bed bath and change of clothing. Tonight, Maryellen arranged for the on-call RN, Heather to come in the early evening to check in.

There are times when my mom is lucid and engaged and times when she’s confused and agitated. When she’s lucid, I try to remember to pass on your messages and let her know she’s in your thoughts. She forgets that she has no strength and gets very frustrated and then melancholy. She sleeps a lot and ends up in uncomfortable positions. In some ways the bed at JBC was better than the hospital bed that we have here. BUT, the hospital bed at the house has an air mattress that automatically inflates and deflates in different places to help prevent pressure sores. While sleeping in a weird position, she pinched a nerve in her neck and it’s quite painful.

Having Joe here is a blessing. Amy is in Essex taking care of the kittens so there’s less upset for them and for Effie. When she calls for me, the adrenaline rush of fear and dread is a bit less knowing there’s someone here to help if needed. The plan is still to accept a bed if there’s one available at McClure this week. I would very much like the opportunity to spend this last bit of time with her as a companion, not as a caregiver.

November 4, 2022: End of Respite and Next Steps

Respite hasn’t been quite what I hoped for either of us. I think I set my expectations too high. I thought this would be spa-like experience for her and I’d get a break from having to worry all the time. The first night I got caught up on sleep and the next morning I was able deep clean her bedroom from ceiling to floor before I went to see her at JBC. The washing machine and I both got a great workout. That was good. 

For her, not so good. She had a rough couple of days after the fluid drain. I expected this but in addition to pain, she was vomiting a lot. She was severely constipated. That didn’t happen last time. Also, the fluid is returning much more quickly. Her doctor at JBC, Charles, wasn’t sure if the vomiting was just a worsening of her condition or maybe a consequence of the constipation. He decided to treat her constipation. He also switched her pain medication from morphine to dilaudid. Since it can be processed more effectively by the kidneys, he thought the side effects might be less intense. Since she’s on respite, not an admitted JBC patient, he must run any changes to meds by her regular care team. He added Atavin, which acts as a sedative to her medication list because she wasn’t sleeping at night and was complaining that the dilaudid wasn’t working for the pain. The vomit has lessened and 2 days of suppositories has helped the constipation. That’s a win. He has been attentive and she likes him. 

The nurses are another story. She has complained nonstop that they are too rough when moving her and that they don’t pay enough attention to her needs. Part of this is nicotine withdrawal and her refusal to wear a patch, but part of her complaints are valid. I don’t think they are too rough. I think she is frustrated by being so weak and unable to do things for herself, so she is uncooperative when she is being moved. I agree that they don’t anticipate her needs. They are used to caring for people that are hours or days away from death and largely unconscious. They are not going to make sure they put her table back into position after moving it or make sure she has fresh water. They are probably even less inclined to do so since she’s been vocal about the quality of care. They do not respond quickly to the call button. After giving someone a suppository, I think you’d be ready and waiting for the call button to sound. At one point it took 4 minutes for them to respond when she needed to use the toilet. 

She is being discharged on Saturday morning to my care. I feel this is unsafe.  I can deal with the 24/7 nature of it and not sleeping but I can’t deal with injuring or killing her because I’m physically unable to move her properly. I’ve been watching the nurses interact with her. There are always two of them to get her out of bed, reposition her in bed, get her on/off the toilet, wipe her bottom, etc. I can’t do that in my own. The only support hospice can provide is to order equipment like a hospital bed and have MaryEllen come once a day, and also an HHA once a day whenever possible. They feel this is adequate. To be fair, they really are giving us all the tools they have at their disposal, it’s just not enough.

The ideal for me would be for her to stay at JBC until a bed becomes available at McClure but JBC will not keep her. Charles, the JBC doc has been communicating with the hospice medical director but not Dr. Clark. This is problematic for me because I do not trust the hospice care team to think outside of their limited resources when considering her ongoing care. They get paid as long as she’s under their direct care. This is one of the main reasons why we kept Dr. Clark as her primary provider. I have been so emotional I’m not thinking clearly and this didn’t even occur to me until late last night. I immediately messaged Dr. Clark. Turns out she is on vacation but her backup called me and said that she would speak with Charles directly. She believes I at least deserve a detailed care plan, medication schedule, and an update on disease progression and prognosis. She said that providing medication as needed 24/7 is not a valid care plan. It makes it impossible for me to figure out a minimal level of additional support I need to care for her safely at home. It is assumed that I will have friends and family available 24/7 to assist with her care. That’s ridiculous. Private pay, if I could find someone good, would be a minimum of 850 a day. That is the equivalent of 2 days of care at McClure. I need to be able to make financially responsible decisions and spend the minimum on caring for her at home so I free up those funds to pay for care at McClure for as long as she needs. I don’t have hope that anything will change by pulling in the backup PCP but I want her condition details documented in her chart before she is sent home from JBC. I also now have it on record and documented in her chart that I believe they are knowingly sending her from JBC to an unsafe environment.  

As it stands now, Joe and the kittens will be moving to Hartland to help me. He thinks he will be able to work from Hartland and that the cats will adjust. I don’t see him being able to work if her needed level of care is the same as it was before JBC. I don’t have a longevity prognosis to work with at this point for coordinating assistance with her care. I do not know when a McClure bed will come through. I am usually a ‘hope for the best and plan for the worst kind of person’ but I’m so tired of planning, I’m just hoping that a bed will be available next week. I believe we are at the top of their list. Unfortunately, they are getting pressure from UVM Med Center to use hospice beds for inpatient overflow from the hospital because the reimbursement rate is much higher than taking someone from a home care situation like my mom. Currently waiting for the hospital bed to arrive.

October 31, 2022: R-E-S-P-I-T-E

The stars aligned. We got a 5-night respite bed at the Jack Byrne Center. I am still a bit in shock. It took me 3 tries to spell shock, because I’ve had under 8 hours sleep since Thursday.  Saturday and Sunday night were particularly bad. She couldn’t get comfortable, couldn’t get herself to a sitting position, couldn’t get herself to/from the toilet, couldn’t get up from the regular toilet so she had to use the bedside commode, couldn’t feed herself because her hands were shaking, and nothing was helping the pain. Sunday night the back pain was joined by severe abdominal pain. She was so distraught and I was helpless to do anything but squirt morphine into her mouth. We did manage to get to the fluid drain appointment at 6:30 AM this morning with much vomiting and a near Derek disaster in the waiting room.  They took out almost 2 gallons of fluid.   

First thing this morning I texted Maryellen, the VNH hospice nurse, and told her that if we didn’t get respite at the Jack Byrne Center at Dartmouth today, I was going to call McClure at UVM and ask for the first available bed. I have never been so tired and so helplessly out of my depth. I just cannot keep her safe and comfortable and its scary and sad and overwhelming and a hundred other feelings I can’t describe. At 10:30 AM Maryellen texted and said there was no beds available for respite at Jack Byrne.  I hemmed on calling McClure. What if the fluid removal made her feel significantly better and she was about to have some of the best remaining days of her life? Wouldn’t she rather be at home for that? I decided to wait until the morphine wore off a bit and I could discuss it with her. While I was waiting, I got a call from Wendy the social worker at VNH. She wanted to talk to me about moving my mom to McClure. I explained that I was on the fence and she told me that I needed to take the next bed that was offered to me. She said that people rarely get a bed when they truly need one and it’s heartbreaking. She said McClure would be a gift compared to a regular nursing home or inpatient hospital. (It better be at $400 per night!) She said she remembered talking to my mom about how she wanted her final days to be. Like many of you, she reminded me that my mom was adamant about my not taking care of her personal needs as her illness progressed and about not wanting to die at home. As she was encouraging me to call McClure, she got a text from her contact at the Jack Byrne Center. They had a respite bed but they were concerned about giving it to my mom because she had the PleurX catheter put in that morning. I’m not sure why that was a show-stopper but I told Wendy that my mom actually decided NOT to do the PleurX. She quickly texted her contact and then told me she would get on a call with JBC and the VNH medical director and call me back. She warned me not to get my hopes up but your prayers paid off and she called me back and said the bed was mine if I could get my mom there by 4PM. When I told my mom, she tried to jump out of her chair to head for the door. I quickly packed her up – I put together a packing list weeks ago – and we headed out an hour later. She vomited the whole way there but she was tucked comfortably in her new bed by 4 PM.

I feel so truly blessed. This experience at the Jack Byrne Center (JBC) is as close to a last vacation as she’ll get. I’m already beyond grateful to Natalie, her RN for the next 2 days, Peggy, their resident chef, and Dr. Charles Whang, their staff physician. Natalie assured me that they would take care of her every need. Natalie’s patient load is 2 so I believe she can take good care of my mom. She is nice without being saccharin and I think my mom will respond well to her. Natalie read my mom’s chart before we got there. When we met with Charles, she was able to recall dates and details that I would have had to look up in my notes, like the exact date of my mom’s last fluid drain. Natalie read that my mom plays water volleyball and told me she wanted to get her into the giant jacuzzi tomorrow because she probably missed the water. They have a nice menu, including all sorts of delicious sounding smoothies, and Peggy will make you literally anything you want to eat. She was quizzing me about my mom’s favorite foods and any cravings she mentioned. My mom, who has zero appetite, couldn’t resist trying a maple smoothie and said it was delicious. Natalie said I was fortunate that Charles is on rotation this week as the MD. She said he is really good. I thought she meant that he was good at interacting with the patients, which he is, but she meant at problem solving and making sure his patients are as comfortable as possible. He immediately suggested that we discontinue morphine because her failing kidneys can’t process it properly and she gets slammed by the side-effects. He recommended we try switching to Dilaudid for pain while she was staying at JBC. He mentioned that he read in her chart that she has a hard time sleeping and that she prefers to manage pain with Tylenol whenever possible because she doesn’t like to be ‘loopy’. He said he thought she would feel more mentally like herself on Dilaudid. He seemed to be a bit surprised about the ongoing cycle of constipation and diarrhea. He said he would look more closely at her history and come up with a plan to address. Natalie said they would get her un-constipated while she was there and my mom said that the last time it was an explosion and Natalie said “We will clean you up, that what we’re here for.”

I don’t know if she will come home on Saturday or be transferred to McClure. That’s a decision to be made after sleep.  Maybe I’m over tired but right now I feel like this is literally the best possible thing that could have (realistically) happened for both of us — even though it hurt like hell to drive her away from her home for what could be the last time.

And on that note, I’m gonna feed Effie and get into bed!

No respite at Jack Byrne in sight. I don’t have any hope that it will happen in the next month.

Monique, her Home Health Aide was out on Thursday and Friday. She will still visit 2x per week after her day job, so around 5PM, and check in via text every morning. Not sure we will ever see Megan again, which is kind of sad for me and maybe a bit for my mom too.

On Friday, I was finally able to get the referrals for the paracentesis (fluid drain) and the PleurX catheter insertion for at home drainage straightened out. I worked with Kat in Interventional Radiology to schedule an appointment, which will be at 6:30 AM on Monday. It was either that ungodly hour or wait an additional 4 days. They can stick to their job description and get you on the schedule at a time convenient for the provider or they can go above and beyond and get you scheduled ASAP.)

After all the back and forth, my mom surprised me later that afternoon by telling Maryellen that she does not want the PleurX catheter. I think just getting in and out of the car might make her collapse from exhaustion, never mind the actual procedure. It was the night we had to have the nurse on call come to the house. She is so much weaker than she was the last time that I think the energy it will take her to recover will outweigh any benefits she might see from the procedure.

Today she is saying her stomach is way too upset to eat. Multiple times throughout the day she’s asked me to help her sit up so she can get out of bed to have a cigarette but then she says she’s too tired to stand up and lies back down and goes right to sleep. She did eat a black cherry yogurt and 5 green grapes. She does not want dinner. I can’t force her to eat. There is no room in her stomach.

If she is still saying she wants to go to McClure on Monday, then I’ll call Ryan and tell him we are ready for bed and then keep my fingers crossed that they have one available shortly.

It is time to say good-bye. She is more miserable than anyone should ever have to be. I think she’s mentally getting herself ready.

One of the reasons we didn’t have her chemo port removed was because we thought it might be helpful down the road for IV fluids and pain meds. The couple of times the oncologist decided she was dehydrated and ordered fluids, she felt immediately better.

Thursday, we decided to also stop the tramadol and go back to trying to manage the pain with Tylenol and heat. She said her quality of life on tramadol just sucked. We also decided to try to get some IV fluids into her. It took me about 6 hours and 4 conference calls to get hospice to agree to make a one-time exception and give her fluids. Unfortunately, Maryellen was dealing with a dying patient and wasn’t able to weigh in on the discussion. The hospice staff finally pulled in her PCP, Dr. Clark. Regardless, she and I make that decision, not someone who has never met or examined her. FFS, before I’ve euthanized a terminally ill pet, I’ve tried procedures to help them maintain their quality of life including removing abdominal fluid and giving subcutaneous fluids. Why would we not do the same for a human – why are things like paracentesis and IV fluids so taboo with hospice? Maybe starting hospice earlier than most people was not a good idea, maybe they assume she is about to die any second. The hospice  people I dealt with on Thursday seemed so focused on supporting the dying process that they would categorically forgo a treatment that would make someone feel better because it just might prolong their life. Drugs are ok because they just make you comfortable and spacey – they won’t make you live longer, but fluids could make you live longer so you can’t have those? So, hospice…. Everyone talks so much about how wonderful hospice can be. I think that is probably largely the case. Hospice probably does attract the most caring and compassionate care providers. What people seem to forget, or maybe what they don’t know, is that almost all hospice agencies these days are for-profit businesses. Medicare pays the hospice agency a per person benefit and the hospice agency is then responsible for paying the bills for whatever that patient needs. Once you’re on hospice, Medicare will not pay for you to see other providers for anything related to your illness unless your hospice agency arranges for the appointment. Hospice has total control over deciding what you ‘need’ for longevity and quality of life. It’s in their financial interest to minimize your expenses for medication, durable medical equipment, physician or hospital visits, and nursing homes. I’m not saying they actually operate their business with a focus on minimizing care expenditures. But I am saying that there is clearly a benefit for them doing so. It’s not like they get paid a bonus from Medicare based on how you perceive your quality of life to be under their agency’s care. They get paid for keeping you alive, at home, for as long as possible. I know that sounds cynical and I’m truly not anti-hospice. I think there’s a time and a place for hospice but I’m seeing it pushed very early in a terminally ill patient’s end-of-life journey and that may not be in the patient’s best interest. From what I’ve seen so far, you need to advocate for yourself constantly to get the care you need for the quality of life you want. You lose a lot of the autonomy to advocate for yourself when you are on hospice.

I’m still writing up notes from my meetings and seminars and verifying the info I receive. I still plan to share it here when I’m done but her are a few juicy tidbits to get you started.

Today I was able to confirm that the money she spent on the wedding last fall (against my wishes) will be considered a gift and she will be penalized by Medicaid. There’s a fun little formula each state uses to calculate your penalty for giving away money before you need long-term care Medicaid. The penalty is a delay in Medicaid eligibility by X days after you meet the financial and clinical criteria for eligibility.

                                Formula: Total amount gifted / state penalty divisor amount = penalty period

In VT the penalty divisor amount is $10,148.35 per month or $344.86 per day.

So, say back in September of 2017 you gave your child $5,000 to help with the down payment on their first home. As of today, April 21, 2022, you need Medicaid to help pay for your long-term care. You’ve spent every cash asset you have on your nursing home bills over the last two months and have around $2,000 left in your checking account. Because you “gave away asset” in the 5 years prior to needing Medicaid, your eligibility for receiving Medicaid will be delayed by a specific number of days, even though you currently meet the clinical and financial requirements for receiving Medicaid as of today.

So, in the scenario above, your eligibility will be delayed by 14.5 days.

Formula: $5,000 / $344.86 = 14.5 days of ineligibility

You will need to figure out how to pay for those 14.5 days in the nursing home before Medicaid will help you out. Remember, you can only have $2,000 in the bank to be eligible for Medicaid, so you’ll need to figure out how to come up with the extra cash to pay the nursing home during your Medicaid penalty period. You better hope you haven’t misplaced that magic wand that turns $2,000 into $5,000.

Oh, and good luck paying your mortgage as a single person when you need long-term care. Unless you’re raking in over $10,000  per month, every penny you have will go to nursing home care. Once Medicaid kicks in, Vermont lets you keep $72 of your monthly income to pay that mortgage and all other home owner expenses. Now your magic wand is really going to need to do some heavy lifting. 

If you think it’s likely you’ll need long-term care (nursing home or in-home nursing) in the next 5 years, DO NOT give any assets away without consulting with an Estate Planner. Any payment to an individual or an ‘unusual’ corporation (e.g., a florist or the American Cancer Society versus Best Buy or Home Depot) during the Medicaid 5 year “look back period” of an amount of $1,000 can be flagged by Medicaid. The burden will be on you to prove that you did not gift an asset. (It’s not unheard of for Medicaid to flag amounts for as little as $500.) Also, if you don’t have family that can pay your mortgage while you’re in long-term care, you should see an Estate Planner and figure out how to put aside some assets so your house doesn’t get foreclosed while you’re in long-term care. And, for the love of cats, don’t forget that Medicaid let’s you keep your house while you need care BUT they will put a lien on your home when you pass so they can recoup their money. Your home is not “safe from Medicaid”.

I do mean to be an alarmist here. In the USA, you must be flat broke before your government helps you pay for long-term nursing care. IF it is important to you that the family you leave behind receives a financial parting gift, start Estate and Long-Term Care planning no later than age 65. Especially if you are single. At a minimum, in most states, you can fairly easily, and inexpensively, set up an enhanced life estate deed (aka Lady Bird Deed) to preserve your home asset for your loved ones.

  • Blood pressure: She should continue to monitor her blood pressure at home. Dr. Clark is not too worried about her BP (140/72).   • Ankle/leg swelling: Dr. Clark did not think there was anything we should do about this. She didn’t consider the swelling severe or symptomatic of blood clots.
  • Other: She should no longer take the daily baby aspirin. The protocols have changed and she doesn’t have a significant risk of stroke or known heart disease, she does not need to take it.

Dr. Clark cautioned her about switching up too many things at one time and to swap the Creon enzyme OR the Citrucel as a starting point. She said that she should give any change at least 2 weeks before making a call on whether it is working.

Regarding end-of-life care….

We talked a lot about whether it was time for hospice care to start. Dr. Clark asked her :

“Consider a scenario in which you’ve caught pneumonia. In this scenario, would you be willing to go to the hospital for a few days IF you could return home feeling like you do today?“

My mom’s response was “Yes” to this question. Dr Clark said that when the answer becomes “No”, then she’s ready for hospice.

For now, we will continue to work with Dr. Clark, her PCP at APD, and Palliative Care at Dartmouth (DHMC). I will look to Palliative to take the care on symptom management. We will periodically proactively reassess the ‘pneumonia scenario’ and switch to hospice when the answer becomes “No”.

Dr. Clark encouraged us to start thinking beyond hospice. At some point she will need to have someone available to address her physical needs all the time. That is not a service that hospice will provide. Hospice is on-call 24/7 for emergencies but they do not come and stay in your home. They will come to your home for scheduled visits and can help with bathing, dressing, and household tasks. There will come a point when she will need personal care 24/7 care. Family can provide this level of care but Dr Clark says it should never be solely one family member. She really emphasized this and it was kind of her to think of me. It certainly crossed my mind as I‘ve been participating in caregiver discussion groups and meetings. Solo caregivers are exhausted by the physical demands and overwhelmed with the burden of making hundreds of what feel like life-or-death micro decisions every week while dealing with their own grief. (If you ever find yourself in a caregiver situation, think twice about joining groups. It’s hard to keep participating and witnessing so much despair but once you join, you can’t really disappear because it’s like you’re abandoning people who need support.)   

Outside of family members, the options for 24/7 care are a nursing home or a private nurse or home health aid. An actual in-patient hospice facility vs nursing home, like the Jack Byrne center at DHMC, is not an option. They only provide care for a few days and they often run out of beds.

The Alice Peck Day geriatric care social worker, Erika Sharbaugh, will reach out to us to discuss options for 24/7 care. I think ultimately, she wants to stay at home and avoid a nursing home. I’m not inclined to leave these big decisions to the last minute and I want to start searching for the right place or right person ASAP. My guess is that this isn’t really a topic she wants to focus on so I’m glad the social worker will help drive additional conversations instead of just me nagging her to discuss.

Private care nursing will likely not be covered by her commercial insurance or by Medicare in our situation. Private nursing is sometimes covered by insurances when there’s an actual chance for recovery. There is a good chance that even a nursing home would not be covered. Medicare only covers a nursing home for care that requires a medical degree to provide. It won’t cover a nursing home when a person just needs assistance with personal care like bathing, dressing, eating, and going to the bathroom. Private insurance will cover a rehab facility only. There may be ways to ‘work the system’ or nuances that I don’t understand in the coverage policies so I’ll start researching.

Consult 1: Dr. Kerrington Smith, Surgical Oncologist

 

Dr. Smith tells us that if the nodules in her lungs are pancreatic cancer, then she’s at stage 4, meaning the cancer has spread/metastasized. Typically, pancreatic cancer spreads to the nearby organs first. He tells us that the typical life expectancy of 2-3 months after a stage 4 diagnosis doesn’t necessarily apply here. That prognosis assumes that the cancer has metastasized to nearby organs.

 

He says next steps should be a lung biopsy and a tumor marker test to quantify the amount of cancer. This test is called Cancer Antigen 19-9 or CA 19-9. Normal range for that test is less than 35. From a surgeon’s perspective if it’s over 1000, it’s a sign that cancer has spread. He cautions us not to rely too heavily on the CA-19-9 test. He says about 10% of people have results in the normal range even when there is cancer throughout their body.

 

She is not a candidate for surgery because the tumor is wrapped around a blood vessel. It would have to shrink considerably to be operable. At this point, while he is technically part of her care team, he really won’t be involved in her treatment ongoing.

 

He suggests that chemo is worth a try to stabilize the tumors and prevent them from growing or to at least slow down their growth. Everyone tolerates chemo differently. If she doesn’t tolerate it well, she can always stop and just do palliative care to manage the symptoms.

 

When asked about clinical trials, he explains that you ‘have to fail first line treatment’ before you can even be eligible for a clinical trial. First line treatment is always chemo.

Consult 2: Dr. Gregory Ripple, Oncologist, Care Team Lead

Dr. Ripple recommends chemo regardless of lung biopsy. If lung biopsy DOESN’T show cancer, then there may be a role for radiation in treatment. He also recommends getting a Mediport inserted in her chest. It’s easier for the doctor to give medication or IV fluids through a port. He tells us the goal of chemo isn’t going to be curing the cancer, it will be extending life by slowing the growth. There are around 7 different types of chemo drugs and they are usually given in combos depending on the type of cancer and the goal for treatment.

He is recommending a two-drug combo treatment of chemo once per week for 3 weeks with a week off between rounds. She will have blood work prior to each treatment to check red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), and platelet counts. If her counts are too low, they won’t be able to do treatment. To assess how the treatment is working, she will continue to get CA 19-9 tumor marker blood work will checked at the start of each cycle, which would be about every 4 weeks with the schedule he is proposing. She will have a CT scan about every 8 weeks or so. If she has symptoms that make them worry that cancer is progressing, we can do the CT scans sooner.

In the absence of chemo in stage v, time would generally be less than 6 months. Probably in the 3-6 month range. With the 2 med approach, on average, time is 10-11 months. It is possible to shorten life expectancy with chemo though this is unusual. 

Treatment duration is not pre-defined. In this situation, there is no limit. Duration is determined by how the cancer is progressing and how she tolerating the chemo.

The drugs he is recommending as her chemo treatment are Gemcitabine and Paclitaxel (Abraxane). These are the side effects he mentioned and this correlates with what I subsequently read online.

      • Generally hair loss, nausea and vomiting – don’t see vomiting a lot.
      • Often see change in appetite, sometimes a change in taste – some people say a metallic taste.
      • Fatigue is very common, often for 3-4 days after treatment. They often give a steroid during chemo to help with the fatigue. *Unfortunately, she doesn’t tolerate prednisone at all, so she will not be able to take the steroid for fatigue.*
        • If fatigue is bad, we may decide to switch to an every other week schedule so she has more ‘feel better’ time between treatments to do things she enjoys. 
      •  Can lower your blood counts, causing bleeding and infection.
        •  If she has a fever and we are told her blood count is low, she has to be seen immediately, even if it means going to the emergency room.
        • Sometimes people will come in to chemo appointments with a blood count that’s too low to go ahead with treatment and we just say we’ll try again next week.
      • Neuropathy and tingling in fingers and toes.
      • Allergic reaction like skin rash. More serious allergic reactions are uncommon.

Most of the negative side effects go away when treatment stops EXCEPT for neuropathy, which can become long lasting. This can be seen with drugs like Paclitaxel.  

After additional research, I learned that there are two forms of Paclitaxel: Abraxane and Taxol. He is recommending Abraxane (also called Nab Paclitaxel).

In general, she finds that patients feel better if they’re doing small meals because it’s less of a burden on their GI tract.
